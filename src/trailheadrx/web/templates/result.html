Find out what your employer's plan does

It may differ from the health plan's default policy.

If your employer covers your benefits, it may have additional rules that apply to your medications.

Everything above comes from the rulebook each health plan publishes. If your coverage is through an employer, the employer may have changed that rulebook — dropped the "try first" steps, added some, taken the medicine off the list, or handed it to an outside "alternative funding" company. Nothing published tells you which, so here is how to find out, easiest first.

  1. Look it up yourself. Your pharmacy benefits card (the one you got at enrollment; it may be separate from your medical card) names a website — Caremark, Optum Rx, Express Scripts, or similar. Sign in and search for {{ job.request.drug }}. The letters next to it are your plan's real rules: PA means your doctor must get approval first, ST means you must try other medicines first, NC means not covered. If you do not have the card, ask your employer's HR or benefits office for the site and your member ID.
  2. Ask one question. Call the member services number on the card, or ask HR: "Does our plan use the standard step-therapy rules for {{ job.request.drug }}, or our own?" If the answer is "our own," ask what they are.
  3. Ask for the plan booklet. HR must give you the plan's official description (the "Summary Plan Description") and its prescription-drug section if you ask; that is federal law. Search it for "step therapy," "prior authorization," and "alternative funding."
  4. Optional: check whether your employer pays its own claims. This matters because Ohio's step-therapy law only protects you if the plan is insured, not if the employer self-funds it. Ask HR, or look up your employer at the U.S. Department of Labor's Form 5500 search (employers with 100 or more people in the plan file one; a "Schedule A" naming an insurer for health benefits usually means insured).

One thing to watch for. If the pharmacy says {{ job.request.drug }} is "not covered" and someone refers you to an outside company that will "help you apply for free medicine from the maker," that is an alternative funding program. Some makers now refuse those applications, so ask HR directly, and get the plan's decision in writing: a written denial is what lets you request an exception and, if refused, appeal. Sources: NASTAD issue brief on alternative funding programs (2025); U.S. DOL, identifying self-insured plans from Form 5500. Checked Sep 20, 2026.

How to use this

This is a plain-words reading of your health plan's own published rules. It is not the final word on your case, and it is not medical advice. Think of it as a map for the conversation with your doctor's office and your health plan: it tells you what they will ask for, how long it may take, and where you have room to push. That is also why we show things like how the same plan treats people in other states, and faster ways to get the medicine your doctor thinks is right for you. Your doctor's office sends in the request; your health plan makes the decision; you can ask questions and ask for exceptions at every step.
